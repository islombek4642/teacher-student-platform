You will hear part of a lecture about archaeological research in the Patagonia region of South America.

Lecturer: Afternoon, everyone. Welcome to today's archaeology lecture. This afternoon's topic will be the Patagon civilization that flourished at the southern tip of the South American continent around 600 years ago. Now, archaeologists don't know a great deal about the Patagon people.

However, they did leave behind one important piece of evidence, a type of coat that was worn over the body for warmth that gives us valuable information about this lost culture. The coat was made from the fur of the guanaco. That's a wild animal like a goat, and it took as many as 13 young guanaco to make a single coat.

31Young animals were preferred as their skin was soft, which made it easy to work with. For the Patagon men, hunting was a way of life, particularly hunting guanaco. However, once the animals were killed, then different skills were needed, and it was the women who were mostly responsible for the manufacturing process.

In particular, it was their ability to sew together many small skins into a coat that made women integral to the process. So, the first thing they did was get the skins ready, and for this they used a special scraper tool. Several examples of the scrapers may still be seen today in various museums in the region. 32They were constructed from specially sharpened stone, so have survived remarkably well.

Once the skins were clean, they were usually decorated. Usually done by painting on colours, mostly some combination of red, yellow, and green. 33The colours themselves occurred naturally in minerals that were dug up from the ground and processed, usually by mixing with water. This basically was the way the Patagon people made coats for, well, over a thousand years and perhaps longer. But the process changed dramatically after the Spanish arrived in the early 1500s.

The coming of the Spanish obviously had numerous impacts on the area. But in terms of coat making, there was one significant development in the hunting technique. 34Many more guanaco were hunted after the introduction of the horse to the region, which made the Patagon people more mobile.

Another important development was that the Spanish themselves liked, were great admirers of the coats and bought them for high prices. So that while in the past guanaco had only been hunted to meet subsistence needs, 35now the coats were increasingly part of a commercial process. I mean, something that could be bought and sold for money. And that meant many more guanaco were hunted as well.

Finally, the manufacturing process itself was changed by the arrival of the Spanish. That's because they bought many new materials with them that weren't previously known in the region. Most important for coat making, the old scrapers used for cleaning the skins 36were replaced with new ones made from glass. And this was more effective and easier to keep sharp.

So what conclusions can we draw from Patagon coats? Well, the striking designs that were painted onto many of the more elaborate coats have always fascinated archaeologists. These patterns must have their origins far back in prehistoric times. We are able to say this with some confidence because 37very similar designs have been found in examples of art painted on the rock walls of caves that have very ancient origins.

Did the designs have any significance or were they merely decorations? The answer isn't clear, but some archaeologists have been prepared to speculate. One possibility is that 38the designs may have represented a way of connecting with ancestors from previous times. And this is certainly an idea that deserves further investigation.

Another interesting speculation concerns the location of large Patagon settlements. Because the natural habitat of the young Guanaco was the rich grasslands of inland areas, 39it seems unlikely that these were constructed around the coast, but rather in parts of the interior.

Finally, we should note that the traditional art of coat making has long since been lost in this region. But 40Patagon shepherds today do still employ some of the same techniques to make blankets in the winter months, though there is no painting or design work involved.

Well, that is the end of my lecture. That is the end of part four.
