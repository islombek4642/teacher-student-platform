Lecturer: Good morning. Last week, I talked about various research methods in the natural sciences, and you did some background reading on that topic. Today, I want to take up that theme again and look at a specific example of how data gained using newer techniques has really pushed our knowledge forward, more or less overturning existing theories. So, I'm going to look at the case of the Red Sea Urchin.

First of all, some background. As the name suggests, this is a marine animal, and it's found in shallow coastal waters. 31It grazes on plants. It's an herbivore, in other words. Here in North America, we can find it all along our west coast, from Alaska down to California. So, many of you will be familiar with it.

And it survives very well because 32it's covered in spines, which are very sharp, and these deter most predators. Now, in the 1960s, in this country, the Sea Urchin was thought to be partly responsible for the sudden decline in kelp, a plant which supports an extensive ecosystem. So, in fact, 33it was considered to be a pest.

Incidentally, since the 1960s, the thinking on that has changed, and humans are now thought to be to blame. But anyway, Red Sea Urchins weren't popular, and people even tried to poison them, to get rid of them in any way possible. But then, in the 1970s, attitudes changed. Interestingly, this had nothing to do with science. There were no new insights into the biology of the Sea Urchin. It was, in fact, a purely economic development.

34It became apparent that there was a demand for the Red Sea Urchin in Asia, where certain parts of the animal were considered to be a great delicacy. And so, a commercial fishery was established based on Sea Urchins. They brought high prices. In fact, at one point during the 1990s, they were one of the most valuable marine resources in the state of California. So, people began to look at the Sea Urchin in a different light. And just at this time, 35people noticed an apparent decline in the numbers of Sea Urchins.

This prompted a wave of scientific studies, with the purpose of getting more information about the species, its life cycle, its biology, survival rate, growth patterns, and so on. So, what did research uncover? Well, the most significant findings came from research based on biochemical techniques. Basically, individual Sea Urchins were tagged and injected with tetracycline, which allowed researchers to track their growth rates.

And it quickly became apparent that the Sea Urchins grew a lot more slowly, and lived a lot longer than had previously been thought. Astonishingly, they can have a vast lifespan, greater than that of almost any other terrestrial or marine species. 36They can live to be 100 years old, and some may even reach 200.

Just imagine, an individual Red Sea Urchin that hatched early in the 19th century may still be thriving, and even breeding. It was found that this animal does grow fairly quickly when it's young. At the age of 2, it can grow from 2 to 4 centimetres in just one year. But even then, according to scientists who conducted the research, it still takes 6 or 7 years before it's big enough to be harvested. By the time the Red Sea Urchin is a teenager, its growth slows quite dramatically, and from the age of 22, when it's about 12 centimetres long, 37it grows at just 0.1 of a centimetre a year. But somehow, the animal never really stops growing.

It just grows incredibly slowly. Some of the largest, and probably the oldest, Sea Urchins were found off the coast of British Columbia, between Vancouver Island and the mainland. And now another very recent study, one using an entirely different technique, has produced independent evidence for these findings.

The study measured levels of radiocarbon, and it has shown that even very old Sea Urchins 38show almost no sign of ageing when something finally kills them. Unlike when someone looks at you or me, you can't look at a Sea Urchin and say whether it's young or middle-aged. And in addition to that, 39even when they're incredibly ancient, they are still able to breed.

So, by now, you may be wondering how, with this phenomenal lifespan, there aren't a lot more Sea Urchins around than there actually are. Well, there is a suggestion that reproduction rates for the species are low, because not many Red Sea Urchins are able to survive the first year of life. The study also confirmed the finding that in older Sea Urchins, there is a very steady, very consistent rate of growth. And 40this growth is independent of ocean conditions and other variables.
